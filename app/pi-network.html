The Pi network

Between the tube and the antenna connector, the ACOM 1010 has a Pi-L network: coils and capacitors whose values you set with three knobs. TUNE and LOAD are its two variable capacitors; BAND switches its coils. This page explains what the network does and why each knob does what it does, assuming you have never met one.

1. The problem: the tube and the antenna want different things

The amplifier's 4CX800A tube makes power as a large voltage swing at a modest current: a swing of well over a thousand volts on its plate. The load that suits it best, the optimum plate load, is roughly the square of that swing divided by twice the power. In the trainer's model that comes to about 2,300 Ω. That is an estimate: ACOM does not publish the 1010's plate voltage, and the club will measure it.

R_opt ≈ (peak plate swing)² / (2 × power) ≈ (1,850 V)² / (2 × 750 W) ≈ 2,300 Ω

The coax and the antenna are about 50 Ω: low voltage, high current. Connect the antenna straight to the plate and the tube would be loaded nearly fifty times too heavily. It would run out of current long before it built up its voltage swing, and would deliver only a small part of its power.

What is needed is the electrical version of a gearbox: something that trades voltage for current without wasting power. At low frequencies a transformer does that. In an HF amplifier that must work from 1.8 to 29.7 MHz and adjust to different antennas, the job falls to a network of coils and capacitors you can tune.

2. The parts: coils and capacitors

Both parts store energy instead of burning it, so they can reshape an impedance with very little loss. Each has a reactance (see the Smith chart tutorial), and it depends on frequency in opposite ways:

Coil: X_L = 2π f L (rises with frequency)
Capacitor: X_C = 1 / (2π f C) (falls with frequency)

3. One step up: the L network

The simplest matching network has two parts in the shape of an L: one in series with the low-resistance side and one in parallel across the high-resistance side. One part of the pair is a coil and the other a capacitor, so their reactances work against each other. Together they make the low resistance look like a higher one, with nothing left over.

How far it steps up depends on Q, the ratio of the series part's reactance to the low resistance:

R_high = R_low × (1 + Q²)
Q = X_series / R_low

For example, to make the antenna's 50 Ω look like 300 Ω takes Q = √(300/50 − 1) ≈ 2.2: a series coil of about 112 Ω reactance, with a parallel capacitor that cancels what is left over.

A single L has one weakness: the resistance ratio fixes Q, so the designer gets no say in it, and Q matters. A higher Q filters out harmonics better but stores more energy in the parts, heats them more, and makes the tuning sharper.

4. The Pi, and the ACOM's Pi-L

A Pi network is two L networks back to back. A capacitor to ground at the tube's side, a coil in the middle, and a capacitor to ground at the far side: drawn out, it looks like the Greek letter π. The first half steps the high plate resistance down to a low "virtual" resistance in the middle of the coil. The second half steps it back up to the output. Choosing how low to go in the middle lets the designer pick the Q.

The ACOM 1010 adds one more coil after the Pi, making it a Pi-L. The extra L section first raises the antenna's 50 Ω to a middle value, so the Pi has a smaller ratio to cover and the whole network filters harmonics better. The manual describes it in one line (Manual §5.5): "The output tank, comprised of LP1, LP2, LL, CP1-CP3, and CL1-CL4, forms a classic Pi-L network and suppresses harmonic frequency emissions."

  • TUNE is the capacitor at the tube's side (CP). The tube's own output capacitance, about 11 pF, sits in parallel with it.
  • Lp is the Pi's coil (LP1 and LP2 in the real amplifier). The BAND switch selects how much of it is used.
  • LOAD is the capacitor at the output side (CL). It is shared: the last part of the Pi and the first part of the L.
  • LL is the L section's coil, in series toward the antenna connector.

5. Follow the impedance through the network

Start at the antenna and walk back toward the tube, one part at a time, the way the tube sees it. A Smith chart (see the Smith chart tutorial) shows each step as a move along one of its circles:

  • A part in series adds reactance only, so the dot moves along a constant-resistance circle.
  • A part in parallel adds susceptance only, so the dot moves along a constant-conductance circle.

6. What each knob does

  • BAND selects the coil taps (switch S1 in the manual's schematic, Manual §5.5). Each band needs very different reactances, as Figure 1 shows. Never turn it while transmitting: hot switching will eventually destroy the band switch (Manual §3.2h).
  • LOAD sets how far the network steps up the antenna's resistance, and so the resistance the tube sees. That is what the TRI reads. Turned right, the plate-load resistance goes down; turned left, it goes up (Manual §4.5c, Fig. 4-1). Try it in Figure 3: the last row's parallel R follows LOAD.
  • TUNE sits right at the plate and cancels whatever reactance is left over there, including the tube's own capacitance. Forward power peaks when nothing is left over, which is why you peak FWD with TUNE. It barely changes the plate's parallel resistance, so it hardly moves the TRI.
  • The presets in Table 4-1 put both capacitors near the right values for a 50 Ω load. A real antenna is not exactly 50 Ω, so the final settings differ a little from the table. That is why you finish by peaking FWD and centering the TRI, not by trusting the numbers.

7. The other job: filtering harmonics

The tube's plate current is not a pure sine wave. It also contains harmonics: energy at two, three and more times the operating frequency, which must not reach the antenna. A Pi-L has coils in series and capacitors to ground, so it passes low frequencies and blocks high ones: it is a low-pass filter as well as a matching network. Only the frequency it is tuned for passes efficiently.

Back to practice: open the trainer's Inside the amplifier card while you tune. The schematic there shows the live capacitor values, and the Smith chart shows where the plate load is.
